Human-likeness: how people move, against the models (11/22/26)¶

Fitting the student to people has used three numbers: solve rate, moves / optimal, and how they change over trials. Many very different solvers can match those. This notebook compares people with candidate models move by move, on the 70 layouts people played (Olieslagers et al. 2023; 42 participants, every attempt, solved or not), to find which measures tell the models apart - and so which to fit on.

experiments/human_profile.py writes one table, a row per move, for people and for each model (10 runs per layout, capped at max(50, 10 × optimal), no giving up):

source what it is
people every attempt, replayed into boards
AND-OR (paper) Olieslagers et al.'s per-board AND-OR distribution, γ 0.07 (its best fit to people's moves)
AND-OR (network) AND-OR ordered by the start network's dest head, γ 0.07 (the apprentice's teacher)
intuition the start network's policy, temperature 1, 10-board memory (the session student)
forward the forward planner on the start network: K8 D4, λ 16, novelty half-life 80
random uniform legal moves, 10-board memory

1. Which moves: chance of an optimal move by distance to the goal¶

A move is optimal if it brings the board one move closer to the goal, worse if it moves it further away. Distance 1 (red's path clear) is left out: people finish by dragging red off the board, which isn't recorded as a slide, so their moves at distance 1 are only the rare ones made instead of finishing.

2. Where they go: revisits and how long ago¶

  • board seen: the move leads to a board already visited in this attempt; undo: straight back to the board before;
  • placement seen: the moved car lands where it has already stood in this attempt. Its age - how many moves ago that car was last there - is the empirical forgetting curve that a fading novelty memory would have to match.

Takeaways¶

  • Optimal moves and new closest approaches mostly measure efficiency. Pooled over all moves, the forward planner looks closest to people on them, but at matched efficiency every model makes optimal moves at about people's rate (0.55-0.61 against 0.58). Profiles by distance to the goal still differ: people stay better than every model in the middle of a puzzle (distance 6-9).
  • People revisit and undo; the memory-bound models cannot. At matched efficiency people return to an earlier board on 10% of moves and undo the last move on 6%, like the two AND-OR models (7-12%, 4-8%). The intuition student and the forward planner, barred from the last 10 boards, almost never do (0-0.6%). The hard 10-board rule is not human; a fading novelty memory, fitted to people's revisits by age, is the natural replacement.
  • People put cars back where they were more than any model (placement seen 0.40 against 0.25-0.34 at matched efficiency): returning a car to an earlier spot is common for them.
  • People rarely move cars outside the AND-OR tree (3-4%), like the network-ordered AND-OR, the intuition student and the forward planner; the paper's AND-OR spreads its lapses over every legal move and moves such cars 10-20% of the time.
  • People slide cars as far as they go (97% of moves, against 72-78% for every model). This may be the interface (dragging a car until it stops) rather than planning; worth checking before fitting on it.
  • Puzzle difficulty agrees only moderately (Spearman 0.45-0.64 across the 70 layouts), best for the two AND-OR models on solve rate.
  • Over practice people improve on every measure a little: more optimal moves (0.47 → 0.53), fewer revisits (0.17 → 0.12), fewer undos, less moving the same car twice, and faster moves (0.80 → 0.64 s median), while which cars they move stays the same.

Caveats: the models are all the start network or no network, one run of 10 per layout; models never give up; the role of a car comes from the paper's tree, which models moves of at most 4 cells and caps its size (0.5% of boards too large).
