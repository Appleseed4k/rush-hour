p2.4: Rules - what the student and people learn, in words (11/24/26)¶

Part of phase 2: p2_summary.ipynb connects this notebook with p2.1-p2.3.

The student is a neural network: it can match people's behaviour, but what it learns is spread over a million weights. This notebook asks whether that learning can be described in human terms - as rules a person could state - and whether people and the student learn the same rules.

The approach follows Butler, Torlak & Popović (2017), who synthesized interpretable strategies for Nonograms: condition-action rules written in a small language of game concepts (pattern: where it applies; condition: when; action: what to do), learned from the game's exact solutions and chosen for coverage and conciseness. Their rules are sound - a nonogram deduction is never wrong. Rush Hour is planning, not deduction: the right move can look wrong locally (moving a car away to make room), so here a rule is scored instead of proven - how often the moves it picks are optimal.

experiments/rule_mining.py writes everything this notebook reads (models/summary/rules/).

Method¶

Concepts come from AND-OR's view of a board (rule_mining.chain): red needs its path to the exit; a car in that path is a depth-1 blocker; a blocker must vacate the path cells it covers, and the cars in the way of its doing so - either way along its lane - are depth 2; and so on (depth 3+ pooled). Every car in this blocking chain has the cells it must vacate.

Predicates on a move (car, direction, steps):

predicate the move...
red, red_exits is red's / solves the puzzle
depth1, depth2, depth3, off_chain moves a car at that depth in the chain / a car outside it
clears leaves the car covering none of the cells it must vacate
min_clear ...and moves the fewest cells that do, in this direction
short_way goes the way that clears in fewer steps (or the only way that can)
frees lets a car one level up the chain clear, which it couldn't before
chain_fewer, chain_more leaves fewer / more cars in the chain
path_fewer, path_more leaves fewer / more cars in red's path
open_side goes toward the side of its lane with more empty cells
max_slide slides as far as it can

Rules: every conjunction of up to 3 predicates or their negations (4,296 rules). A rule fires on every legal move that satisfies it. Precision: per board, the share of the moves it fires on that are optimal (breadth-first search), averaged over the boards it fires on; chance: the same for all legal moves on those boards.

Decision list: greedily, the rule with the best precision (less 2 points per condition past the first - the conciseness cost) that fires on at least 2% of the boards no earlier rule fires on; then the next, on the boards left.

Boards, all from pool puzzles of 4-20 moves (none of people's layouts):

  • uniform: 60 boards sampled from each of 300 puzzles' whole state spaces (17,863 boards);
  • visited: the boards the two-mode student (start network, its current weights) reaches in 4 attempts at each of the same puzzles (28,141 boards) - the positions a learner actually meets.

Following the rules: on every board people played 2+ moves from the goal (62,251 moves), the first rule of the visited list that fires picks the "rule moves"; a person follows it if they played one of them. For the student, the policy's probability on the rule moves, averaged over the 8 students of the current two-mode session (lean_people_all_m10_ao_nodest_refit) at its start and after 18, 36 and 54 trials.

  • One idea runs through the lists: clear a car in the blocking chain so that the chain shrinks, or so that the car above it can leave. chain_fewer, clears and frees make up almost every rule that covers many boards; as a hint a person could use: find the cars that block red and the cars that block those, and move one so that the set gets smaller.
  • Near the goal the rules are close to sound, as Butler et al.'s are: "drive red out" and "move red's blocker so red's path opens" are never wrong, and the chain rules are 94-100% optimal 1-3 moves out.
  • Far from the goal they reach their limit: 48-82% at 10+ moves (chance 27-39%), where the right move is often one that only looks right after search.
  • Boards a learner actually meets are harder. On visited boards the list needs deeper chains ("depth 3 & clears & chain fewer") and its main rules are less precise (77-87%, against 89-95% on uniform boards): a student wanders into tangled positions that uniform sampling rarely produces, and 14% of visited boards have no rule that fires.
  • Which side of the lane a car goes to matters only a little (below, on visited boards): "move a red blocker so the chain shrinks" is 76% optimal; toward the tight side 80%, toward the open side 75%; the short way 78%, the long way 73%. On uniformly sampled boards the side made no difference (95-96% either way). An earlier list put "toward the side with less room" in a rule of its own: the greedy search choosing a slightly more precise slice of an already precise rule - which is why the list now charges for every extra condition.

  • Both follow the rules far above chance, and more with practice (a). People go from 37.5% of their moves to 42.4%. The student's policy, snapshot by stage, goes from 34.1% to 38.8%, 41.4% and 42.3% - after its start, within 0.1 points of people at every stage. By this measure the student learns what people learn, at their pace, without ever being told the rules: rule-following emerges from learning optimal moves.
  • Near the goal people already know the key rule; the student never quite does (b, c). "Move red's blocker so red's path opens" is people's move 91-93% of the time from their first trials; the student's policy puts 79% on it at the start and 82% after 54 trials. Phase 2's near-goal gap (people near-perfect close to the goal, the student middling) is largely this one rule.
  • People's learning is in freeing the car above. Their clearest gain is on "clears & chain fewer & frees" (47% of moves in trials 1-9, 50% after, 53% from trial 28); the student gains there too (36% → 44%).
  • Far from the goal the student follows the rules more than people do (36% against 30%): trained on hindsight-optimal labels, it drifts toward rule moves where people may be exploring or planning in ways these rules don't express.
  • Deep-chain moves stay rare for both: "depth 3 & clears & chain fewer" is 24-30% of people's moves and 22-25% of the student's - the moves that need seeing several links down the chain.

The student's 8 copies, which start from the same network, can't show differences like these.

4. What the rules are, and what they are for¶

Right now the rules are a lens, not part of the student. Nothing in the student uses them; they describe, in words, what its learning (and people's) amounts to. That the student comes to follow them as people do, unprompted, says the rules capture something real about the task, and that the network has partly learned it in its own terms.

Ways they could become part of the student, from least to most change:

  1. a rule mode in the move-by-move mixture (beside intuition, flat planning and AND-OR), with its weight fitted to people's moves - the test of whether people use rules rather than rules merely describing good play;
  2. rules as a readable bias on the policy: the network's score plus a learned weight per rule that fires, so learning splits into named rules and what the network does beyond them;
  3. learning rules: a production-rule learner that keeps or makes rules from the hindsight labels of its solved attempts (as ACT-R's production compilation, or Butler et al.'s synthesis run during the session);
  4. rules as a teacher's hints: saying a rule instead of showing a move - a middle level of help for the assistance dilemma.

Caveats

  • People are scored by the moves they made, the student by its policy's probabilities (intuition, not the two-mode student's moves).
  • The rules are judged against optimal moves, not human strategies: a person can follow a sensible strategy these rules can't express.
  • The vocabulary is mine and the search stops at 3 conditions; a richer language might cover the 14-22% of boards where no rule fires.
  • Rules that fire on few of people's moves have noisy per-stage numbers.
