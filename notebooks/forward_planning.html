Forward planning for the student: novelty and relevance (11/21/26)¶

The fitted student plays by intuition alone: on every board it samples a move from its policy head (MODEL.md). AND-OR's backward reasoning is only its teacher in pretraining. The question here is whether a heuristically guided forward planner - short rollouts, judged by how much new and goal-relevant ground they cover - is a good candidate for what people add as they gain expertise, and where expertise would enter it.

Two ingredients:

  • Novelty (Lipovetzky & Geffner's width-based planning): a feature is one car in one place on its lane; a rollout earns credit for features no board of the attempt has had yet.
  • Relevance: the network's dest head scores every feature on the current board by how soon a solution from here puts that car there (target 0.8^(k-1) at k moves into the route, 0 if never); r(f) = sigmoid(dest). It is "how likely this new position is to be on the way to the goal".

This notebook reads tables written by experiments/dest_quality.py and experiments/forward.py (models/summary); sections 1.3 and 1.4 compute their tables here.

1. Is the relevance map any good, and does it sharpen with experience?¶

Truth comes from breadth-first search: for every board, the optimal-route DAG, and for every feature the fewest moves k at which some optimal route first puts that car there (none if no optimal route does). Boards: the 100 held-out puzzles of the AND-OR apprentice (1-30 moves), each start plus 2 boards 3 random moves off it and 2 boards 8 random moves off it (500 boards). About 35% of a board's ~40 features lie on some optimal route.

Measures (per board, then averaged; AUC 0.5 = chance):

  • on-route AUC: features on some optimal route vs features on none;
  • next-move AUC: features an optimal next move reaches (k = 1) vs the rest;
  • far AUC: features first reached 3+ moves on vs off-route ones - the long-range part rollouts would need;
  • policy optimal: share of boards where the policy head's top move is optimal (the proposals, for comparison).

2. The forward planner¶

experiments/forward.py, solve_forward. Every turn, on board b:

  1. relevance: the dest head on b gives r(f) for every feature;
  2. proposals: K rollouts of up to D moves, each move sampled from the policy head at temperature 1 (never back to one of the last 10 boards of the attempt or to a board earlier in the rollout, if it can avoid it);
  3. evaluation: a rollout that solves the puzzle wins (the shortest); otherwise its score is the sum of r(f) over the features it reaches that no board of the attempt has had, each counted once;
  4. the first move of the best rollout is played (the first sampled, on a tie).

How strict novelty is. Two mechanisms, only one of which forbids anything:

  • Hard, over whole boards (the session student's own rule): a move back to one of the last 10 boards of the attempt is removed from the options - for the move played and for every rollout step - and, inside a rollout, so is a move back to any of its earlier boards; if every move would, all are allowed again.
  • Soft, over features (the planner's score): no feature is forbidden. A feature seen anywhere in the attempt is worth 0 from then on (no fading); a new one gets its full relevance. It only ranks the K rollouts: if none reaches anything new, they all score 0 and the first sampled one is played - a plain policy sample (the "ties" below). A pruning version, as in Iterated Width, would instead discard such rollouts.

K = 1 is the session student's intuition exactly. Test: 100 of the session's held-out puzzles (25 per bin of 9-11, 14-16, 19-21 and 24-26 moves), 2 runs each, cap max(50, 10 × optimal), no giving up. Score: moves/optimal with an unsolved puzzle counted at its cap (lower is better). Networks: the start, round 3 of pretraining, and the 8 students of the no-teacher and random-100% teachers after 720 puzzles (same session run as 1.2).

Conditions:

  • novelty only: every r(f) = 1;
  • no attempt memory: a feature counts as new if it isn't on b (the current board), so relevance acts as a pure lookahead evaluator;
  • uniform: rollout moves drawn uniformly from the legal ones, so the network acts only through relevance (and "uniform, novelty only" uses no network at all);
  • K16 D8: more and deeper rollouts;
  • value: rollouts ranked by the value head's moves to go at their end board (fewest first), no novelty;
  • novelty then value: novelty count first, the value head breaking ties (best-first width search ranks by novelty, then a goal heuristic).

One score: novelty and value combined, with fading memory¶

"Novelty, then value" ranks strictly: value only matters when novelty ties. A person would more plausibly fold both into one judgment ("I haven't been here before, so I must be getting somewhere"):

score = λ × novelty − moves to go (value head, at the rollout's end)

λ is how many moves of predicted progress one new feature is worth: λ = 0 is value alone, λ → ∞ is the strict order. Novelty can also fade: a feature last on the attempt's boards age moves ago counts 1 − 0.5^(age / half-life) (1 if never seen, 0 on the current board), so a placement seen long ago detracts less than one seen recently; an infinite half-life never forgets (all runs before this one).

  • The combined score costs nothing once λ is about 8 or more. At λ 8 it is level with the strict order (students +0.01 ± 0.02 and +0.07 ± 0.05; start and round 3 within ±0.3), and flat up to 32. Below 4 it loses (λ 2: +0.3 to +0.7): value then outweighs novelty and the planner circles as value alone does. So the human-like, compensatory version is viable, provided a new placement counts for a lot.
  • No clear sign that experience moves the best λ. Every network is best somewhere in 8-32. The start loses most at low λ (+0.71 at λ 2, against +0.33 to +0.39), as if it should trust its value head least - but its value head's scale is inflated (1.4), so the same λ weighs novelty less for it; the units are not comparable across networks.
  • Fading memory is free over tens of moves, harmful over a few. A half-life of 80 moves is at least as good as never forgetting (λ 16: students −0.12 ± 0.05 and −0.11 ± 0.07, start −0.22 ± 0.25); 20 is about level; 5 hurts (+0.08 to +0.20). Performance therefore does not pin the half-life down within a wide range - people's revisits must (when, after leaving a placement, they come back to it).

What the planner shows¶

  • Search with attempt-level novelty roughly halves moves/optimal for every network, and more search (K16 D8) helps further. The gain over intuition shrinks as the network gains experience (−2.9 at the start, −1.3 at round 3), mostly because intuition itself gets better.
  • All of the network's contribution comes through proposals. Policy instead of uniform proposals is worth 2.5-3.3; relevance is worth nothing measurable, whether the policy proposes (where it could be redundant) or uniform proposals leave it the network's only input. Without attempt memory, relevance as a lookahead evaluator is no better than intuition.
  • Blind novelty search is about as good as a novice's instinct (5.64 vs the start's 5.98); the two together halve the moves.
  • A board evaluator helps where relevance could not: on novelty's ties. The value head alone is a weak guide (better than intuition, 0.8-1.4 worse than novelty: greedy on a noisy value circles). Breaking novelty's ties with it - the turns where no rollout reaches anything new - helps a little with policy proposals (−0.1 to −0.4) and a lot with uniform ones (5.64 → 4.2-4.5). Summed relevance cannot act on those turns: every rollout scores 0. Its gain is about the same for every network, so the value head is not where experience shows up either.
  • Strict or compensatory makes no difference to performance once a new feature is worth 8+ moves of value, and novelty can fade over tens of moves at no cost: both choices are for people's data to make.
  • Novelty search is strong enough that adding it to the start student would break the fitted start: a novice's search budget would have to be near zero and grow with practice.

2b. Forward and backward together¶

People's revisits (notebooks/human_likeness.ipynb) ask for no novelty and a soft board memory (β 2, half-life 1), so from here the planner ranks candidates by the value head alone. Backward reasoning joins as a second source of candidates: each turn, backward=N adds N AND-OR passes from the current board (ordered by the dest head with Gumbel noise, lapse rate γ) to the k policy rollouts - a whole plan to the goal, or the moves made until a lapse. All candidates are judged alike (a solving one wins; otherwise the end board's predicted moves to go) and the first move of the best is played.

  • The two sources complement each other. At the same 8 candidates per turn, 4 rollouts + 4 AND-OR passes beat 8 rollouts (by 0.25-0.56 moves / optimal) and 4 passes (by 0.4-0.8) for every network, and backward plans win about half the turns. More passes help backward reasoning alone (4 vs 1: −0.65 to −1.3).
  • Without novelty the planner is much weaker on these puzzles than the novelty planner above (start: 4.88 against about 2.7): the price of matching people's revisits.
  • On the human layouts (human_likeness.ipynb's table and the shared-γ fit below) the mix is efficient (1.85 × optimal, people 2.09) but restless: it returns to an earlier board on 13.6% of moves and undoes 9% (people 9.7%, 5.6%), because the soft memory shapes only the policy rollouts - each turn's fresh AND-OR pass can resolve a subgoal differently and move a car straight back.

  • γ cannot fix the shape of people's skill. Every setting is too good near the goal (99% optimal moves 2-3 moves out, people 87%; 86% even in their first 10 trials) and too poor mid-puzzle (31-43% at 7-9 moves out, people 54%, 48% in their first 10 trials). γ trades these off only a little. Near the goal people make many moves that leave the distance unchanged (10% of their moves there); mid-puzzle they choose better than the start network's value head lets the planner. A lapse at the level of the move played, and a better value head, are the candidates.
  • The share of turns won by backward plans stays at 44-49% whatever the γs.

3. How this relates to the papers¶

  • van Opheusden et al. 2023 (four-in-a-row): best-first search over a feature-weighted value; expertise is mostly more search (depth 5 → 6) and fewer attention lapses (feature drop rate 0.23 → 0.17), with little change in heuristic quality. Consistent with relevance not mattering and search budget helping.
  • Collins et al. 2026 (new games): novices are a flat, one-step, goal-directed Boltzmann policy plus a few cheap simulations - our intuition student. Their per-player mixture of flat / deep / random players is a way to measure a shift toward search in people's data.
  • Zhang et al. 2025 (failures of consideration): an imitation-trained network seeds forward Monte Carlo search and decides what gets considered; undervalued actions are never sampled. Matches "experience enters through proposals".

4. Open questions¶

  1. Relevance in proposals rather than evaluation: bias rollout moves toward relevant features.
  2. Novelty as pruning (Iterated Width): discard rollouts that reach nothing new and resample or search deeper, instead of falling back to a policy sample.
  3. People: does AND-OR explain people's moves less well over trials, or does a forward component grow with practice (a mixture fit per trial bin)?

Caveats: one start network, one round-3 network, one session run (seed 2); the session's held-out puzzles only; the sum score favours rollouts that move many cars.
